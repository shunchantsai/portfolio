# Cascading Failure on the Bhote Koshi: A Catastrophe-Risk Case Study of the 2026 Rasuwa Flood

---

## Executive Summary

On 26 August 2026, a large, rapid slope failure near the Nepal–China border sent rock, ice and sediment into the Lhende Khola and the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli system. The resulting debris flood reached the Rasuwagadhi border area approximately seven minutes after the 08:37 seismic event, at around 08:44 NPT, and propagated downstream through a corridor of settlements, roads and hydropower infrastructure. Gauge records further down the Trishuli show water-level rises of approximately nine metres at Galchhi and seven metres at Malekhu within about thirty minutes. The broad sequence of downstream propagation is well supported, although the precise timing of the initial slope failure and some intermediate processes remain unresolved.

The research question is: what would an insurer, reinsurer, or public risk-finance institution need to understand about the catastrophe risk exposed by the August 2026 Nepal–China border flood-debris cascade?

The central problem is establishing which physical processes caused the damage, what assets were exposed, how much loss can be attributed to the event, and how those losses are distributed among asset owners, insurers and public institutions — when key links in the hazard chain remain unresolved and the available figures come from different scopes, dates and valuation bases.

This case study reconstructs the event under an explicit uncertainty framework. Statements are tagged CONFIRMED, REPORTED or HYPOTHESIS. A large, rapid slope failure and the entry of material into the drainage network are CONFIRMED, although the timing of the initial failure remains unresolved. Rapid downstream propagation is strongly supported by seismic, hydrological and situational evidence. Post-event satellite analyses identify two newly formed water bodies interpreted as barrier lakes; the mapping is preliminary and not field-validated. The hypothesis that a temporary upstream dam formed and breached during the main cascade remains open. Volume estimates and the precise source peak are likewise left unresolved.

An illustrative exposure–vulnerability–loss chain is then implemented. The exposure table is synthetic and incomplete by design; the vulnerability functions are literature-informed placeholders, not Nepal-calibrated curves. The resulting ground-up loss is a model output for the included assets only. External benchmarks are retained as separate reference points and are not used as calibration targets (Table 3; Section 3.5).

The available evidence points to a potentially substantial protection gap. National damage and reconstruction estimates substantially exceed the losses visible in available insurance claims snapshots, but the figures differ in reporting date, coverage, valuation basis and loss definition and cannot be treated as a direct measure of the uninsured share. Hydropower represents an important concentration of formal insurance exposure; public infrastructure, businesses, households and informal livelihoods face losses that may fall outside commercial arrangements. Section 3.4 applies hypothetical deductibles and limits to the synthetic table to illustrate the translation from ground-up to insured loss. Actual policy terms are not modelled.

The case study demonstrates how hazard evidence, exposure assumptions, vulnerability functions and insurance information can be assembled into an auditable analytical framework, and identifies the additional evidence required before the results could support underwriting or public risk-finance decisions.

## 1. The Event and the Hazard Chain

### 1.1 Event overview

At 08:37:10 Nepal Time on 26 August 2026, the USGS recorded a magnitude-5.2 landslide-type seismic event associated with the Rasuwa disaster (USGS 2026). The signal is generally interpreted as marking a large, rapid slope failure near the Nepal–China border, involving rock, ice and sediment entering the Lhende Khola and the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli river system. However, an alternative reconstruction places the initial slope failure on the afternoon of 25 August and interprets the 26 August seismic event as the failure of a temporary blockage. The timing and sequence of these processes remain unresolved. 

The resulting debris flood reached the Rasuwagadhi border area at approximately 08:44 NPT, around seven minutes after the seismic event, covering roughly 20–23 kilometres downstream. It then propagated through the corridor. At Galchhi, the Trishuli gauge recorded a water-level rise of approximately nine metres within about thirty minutes; at Malekhu, the rise was approximately seven metres over a similar period.

A large, rapid slope failure and the entry of rock, ice and sediment into the drainage network are **CONFIRMED**. However, the timing of the initial failure relative to the 26 August seismic signal, and whether a temporary blockage formed and subsequently failed, remain unresolved. Rapid downstream propagation and the gauge response are **strongly supported** by hydrological monitoring and multiple independent situational sources. The precise mechanism responsible for every segment of the flood hydrograph is **not uniformly confirmed**.

Figure 1 summarises the proposed hazard sequence, distinguishing processes supported by the available evidence from those that remain hypothetical.

The 2021 Chamoli rock-ice avalanche (Shugar et al. 2021), the 2016 Bhote Koshi GLOF (Cook et al. 2018) and the 2021 Melamchi debris flood (Chen et al. 2025) are high-mountain analogues for rapid, infrastructure-damaging flows in steep valleys. None is evidence for the mechanism or intensity of the 26 August 2026 event, and none is used as a calibration source.

### 1.2 Source location and volume

Two peaks sit roughly four kilometres apart in the same massif: Langtang Lirung (7,234 m) and Tsangbu Ri (also called Kimshung, 6,781 m). The USGS event page places the signal near Langtang Lirung without specifying flank or elevation. An EarthArXiv preprint argues for failure on the northern flank of Langtang Lirung. Adhikari, Subedi et al. (*Seismica*, October 2026) place a rock fall with some ice, on the order of 100 million cubic metres, on the north flank of Tsangbu Ri, with a relocated seismic point argued to be consistent with a scarp visible on post-event imagery. The accounts have not been reconciled. The volume, ice content and Tsangbu Ri flank attribution appear only in the *Seismica* paper; the northern-flank Langtang Lirung claim appears only in the preprint. Neither is a shared finding. The event location and downstream corridor are shown in Figure 2, providing the geographical context for the exposed settlements and infrastructure discussed below.

The map shows the approximate source area near the Nepal–China border, the downstream river system and the principal locations discussed in this case study. A seismic source location and a visible detachment scarp are not necessarily the same geographical point. Without published relocation uncertainty and the actual spatial separation between the seismic solution and the scarp, the cross-check cannot be weighed quantitatively here. For downstream risk analysis the distinction between the two peaks is secondary: both locations feed the same drainage. For forensic hazard reconstruction it remains open and is carried as **REPORTED**.

Published volume estimates diverge and are left unresolved. The *Seismica* paper reports approximately 100 million cubic metres. Park (EarthArXiv, September 2026) reconstructs a moving volume of 27 (18–35) × 10⁶ m³ with an ice fraction of 0.1–0.3 and presents a reconstruction in which a rock–ice avalanche, without requiring stored water or a temporary dam breach, can reproduce the observed timing and gauge response. That analysis is a non-peer-reviewed preprint.

These figures should not automatically be treated as measurements of the same quantity. Detached source volume, mobilised avalanche volume, and deposit volume (including entrained material) can differ substantially; ice content and bulk-density assumptions also affect comparison. Until definitions, spatial boundaries and treatment of entrainment are reconciled, the numerical difference cannot be interpreted simply as disagreement about the size of the event. No single figure is adopted here. Treating volume as settled would propagate false precision into any subsequent intensity or loss calculation.

### 1.3 Secondary hazards and the temporary blockage hypothesis

Separate post-event satellite analyses identify two newly formed water bodies interpreted as barrier lakes. UNOSAT’s product *Satellite-Detected Newly Formed Barrier Lakes Following an Ice-Rock Avalanche in Rasuwa District, Nepal, as of 28 August 2026* (Cartosat-3) maps these features. Complementary analysis by India’s National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC), reported by The Times of India, used Cartosat-3 imagery from 28 August and pre-event PlanetScope imagery from 25 August to identify two impoundments with estimated surface areas of approximately 19 ha and 11 ha (Reddy, 2026). A rapid satellite assessment of the cascading disaster was also published by Roy et al. (2026). Chinese authorities and Nepal’s NDRRMA also recorded and monitored barrier lakes in the corridor. The mapping is preliminary and not field-validated. Presence of the water bodies is well supported by remote sensing; depth, volume, stability and exact formation timing are not. These post-event lakes are treated as a **satellite-detected secondary hazard**, distinct from any temporary impoundment during the main cascade.

The hypothesis that failed material temporarily impounded a reach (commonly framed as the Lhende Khola or an adjacent channel) and that a subsequent breach amplified the main flood wave remains **HYPOTHESIS**. Temporary blockage and breach have appeared in published scientific and institutional interpretations, including early reconstructions that invoked natural-dam failure as part of the sequence. ICIMOD identified the possibility as a priority line of investigation. Later secondary accounts described barrier-lake formation and breach in more definite terms, including specific travel-time claims.

An institution can publish an expert interpretation; that interpretation can be scientifically plausible; it does not automatically mean that the temporary dam and its breach have been independently demonstrated through field evidence or a sufficiently resolved reconstruction. The existence, location, duration and breach dynamics of a temporary impoundment *during* the 26 August cascade have not been established to the evidentiary standard adopted here. The hypothesis is therefore retained rather than treated as a confirmed link in the hazard chain. Post-event barrier lakes must not be conflated with that unresolved upstream question.


### 1.4 Implications of unresolved hazard mechanisms for catastrophe modelling

For emergency response, the difference between a pure rock–ice avalanche and a cascade that included a temporary dam may be secondary once the wave has already passed. For catastrophe-risk analysis the difference is material. An amplifying breach changes the shape and timing of the hydrograph, peak intensity at downstream assets, and the credibility of any scenario set built from the event.

The temporary-dam question, together with unresolved source volume, ice content and source location, potentially affects:

1. **Hazard intensity** — peak discharge, flow depth, velocity and sediment concentration  
2. **Spatial footprint** — which downstream locations are affected and to what degree  
3. **Temporal characteristics** — arrival time, duration and shape of the flood wave  
4. **Vulnerability response** — how different infrastructure and building types experience the resulting physical conditions  
5. **Financial loss** — the resulting damage estimates and the uncertainty around them  

In a fully developed catastrophe model, these uncertainties would be represented through alternative hazard scenarios, intersected with exposure data and passed through appropriate vulnerability functions. This case study identifies where the uncertainties enter that modelling chain but lacks the hydraulic and asset-level data required to quantify the alternatives. Subsequent calculations are therefore kept separate from estimates of actual event loss.

## 2. Why This Corridor Concentrates Risk

The Bhote Koshi–Trishuli corridor is not merely the path taken by the debris flood. It is a concentration of physical exposure, critical infrastructure and assets with potentially significant insurance and reinsurance implications. Its terrain, cross-border setting and linear infrastructure network help explain why a single high-altitude failure produced impacts across multiple settlements and economic sectors. They also provide the geographical context for the exposure, vulnerability and insurance analysis that follows.

### 2.1 Terrain, confinement and flow concentration

In its upper reaches and other tightly constrained valley sections, the corridor is narrow and steep. Material entering the Lhende Khola and Bhote Koshi therefore has limited opportunity to spread laterally across a broad floodplain. The steep channel gradient may support rapid downstream propagation, while confinement can concentrate the flow through settlements and infrastructure nodes.

The short reconstructed travel time indicates rapid downstream propagation, but average travel time between two points is not equivalent to the local velocity of the debris or water. It is also sensitive to uncertainty in the event start time and first observed impact. 

More importantly, steepness and confinement do not by themselves establish that peak discharge, depth and velocity remained high at any particular downstream cluster of assets. Actual behaviour also depends on channel geometry, entrainment, deposition, flow resistance, temporary obstructions, tributary inflows and the evolving mixture of water and sediment. Discharge, depth and velocity are not interchangeable intensity measures. For catastrophe modelling, the terrain provides a strong basis for identifying locations where flow may be concentrated, but it is not a substitute for estimating local hazard intensity. That would require hydraulic reconstruction.

### 2.2 Cross-border setting and warning asymmetry

The initiating failure and the first tens of kilometres of the pathway sit on or across the Nepal–China border. Upstream monitoring, imagery and institutional response are therefore split across two systems. In this event, the 08:37 seismic event was followed by flood impact at Rasuwagadhi at approximately 08:44. Gauges in the upper Nepali reach ceased transmission as the wave arrived. The Department of Hydrology and Meteorology's Flood Forecasting Division reportedly issued its first alerts at 09:15 and dispatched 679,295 SMS messages by 09:16 (Belbase, 2026). Mongabay’s subsequent report records the same DHM figures but notes that it could not independently verify the timing or recipient count; it also reports a parliamentary statement placing the first SMS batch at 09:35 (Belbase, 2026; Joshi, 2026).

The result was a pronounced warning asymmetry. Communities closest to the cross-border source had little or no practical warning time, while the national warning system issued alerts that could, in principle, reach some downstream communities after the surge had already entered Nepal. Warning lead time therefore varied substantially along the corridor. For risk analysis this is a spatially differentiated **consequence modifier**, not a uniform characteristic of every exposed asset and not a parameter of a building’s physical vulnerability function. Warning time may affect evacuation, human safety, emergency response, contents and movable-property outcomes, business interruption and recovery; it does not rewrite a depth–damage relationship. Assets at the border and in the first corridor settlements faced a near-zero practical warning window for this class of cascade; assets further downstream did not face the same condition.

### 2.3 Infrastructure, settlement and asset concentration

Roads, bridges, border facilities and hydropower works are aligned along the same narrow valley floor that carries the flood. The Rasuwagadhi crossing, the Syabrubesi–Timure settlements, multiple hydropower intakes, powerhouses and tunnels, and successive road and bridge links form a linear accumulation of high-value and high-criticality assets.

Copernicus Emergency Management Service (EMSR927) grading for the two areas of interest (AOI01 Syapru Besi, 346 ha; AOI02 Timure, 297 ha) delineated 237 ha of mass movement and graded 864 building points. The assessment classified the Langtang Khola 20 MW powerhouse, the Rasuwagadhi hydropower dam, six bridges, a helipad and approximately 9.5 km of primary road, including a section of the Pasang Lhamu Highway, as destroyed (Copernicus EMSR927, grading products v1 for AOI01 and AOI02, released 27 August 2026). These are rapid image-based damage classifications, not engineering inspections or estimates of replacement cost. The AOIs are not the full corridor, but they illustrate the density of exposure where the valley is tightest.

Hydropower deserves separate emphasis. According to a preliminary assessment by the Independent Power Producers’ Association, Nepal (IPPAN), the flood damaged 14 hydropower and solar projects across Rasuwa and Nuwakot, comprising nine operational projects with a combined capacity of 354 MW and five under-construction projects with a further 394 MW (Setopati, 26 August 2026). These figures describe the installed capacity of affected projects, not the amount of generating capacity simultaneously shut down. Hydropower assets concentrate replacement value, potential business-interruption exposure and, where covered, formal risk transfer. They also concentrate people. Corridor risk is therefore not only residential and transport risk; it is energy-infrastructure and workforce risk stacked on the same channel.

Asset replacement value, insured value and recoverable insurance loss are separate quantities; the last depends on policy coverage, limits, deductibles and applicable terms.

Dataset metadata that attribute the event to a glacial lake outburst flood are not adopted here as a resolved mechanism; Section 1 leaves key links in the hazard chain open.

### 2.5 Implications for corridor-level catastrophe-risk modelling

Conventional catastrophe-risk modelling follows a sequence from hazard to exposure, vulnerability, physical damage and financial loss. In a geographically concentrated infrastructure corridor, however, asset-level loss estimates alone may not capture the wider consequences of a common event.

Three related but distinct dimensions are therefore relevant to corridor-level catastrophe-risk assessment.

**Geographic accumulation** arises when multiple assets are exposed to the same physical event. A single flood-debris cascade can affect hydropower facilities, bridges, roads, settlements and other infrastructure within the same drainage corridor. This creates the potential for correlated physical damage across asset classes and, where assets are insured, across insurance portfolios. The significance of this accumulation depends not only on the number and value of exposed assets, but also on their spatial distribution relative to the hazard pathway.

**Functional interdependency** arises when the operation, accessibility or recovery of one asset depends on another. Damage to a bridge or access road, for example, may restrict access to a hydropower facility, delay repairs or prevent the restoration of electricity generation even where the facility itself has sustained limited physical damage. The resulting operational interruption can therefore extend beyond the assets directly damaged by the hazard. Assessing these effects requires information on infrastructure dependencies, service functionality, alternative routes and outage duration.

**Financial allocation and risk transfer** concern how the direct and indirect consequences of the event are distributed across different parties and financing arrangements. Asset owners may bear uninsured physical damage or business interruption; commercial insurers and reinsurers may cover eligible insured losses; public authorities may finance infrastructure repair; and households and informal businesses may rely on their own resources or external assistance. The party bearing the physical damage is not necessarily the party bearing the full economic or financing consequences.

These dimensions are connected, but they are not interchangeable. Geographic accumulation concerns the spatial concentration of exposed assets; functional interdependency concerns the transmission of disruption between assets and services; and financial allocation concerns how the resulting costs are distributed and transferred.

For the August 2026 event, the evidence establishes the geographical conditions for accumulation and identifies plausible infrastructure dependencies. However, the operational and financial effects of those dependencies have not been quantified. The present loss calculation therefore captures an illustrative direct physical-damage component only. A fuller corridor-level assessment would need to link spatially distributed hazard intensity and asset vulnerability with infrastructure functionality, interruption losses and the financial arrangements through which losses are transferred or retained.

## 3. Exposure, Vulnerability, and Illustrative Loss

This section implements the exposure–vulnerability–loss chain on a synthetic asset table, applies hypothetical insurance terms, and sets the results beside external figures.

### 3.1 Nature of the exposure table

The exposure inventory is synthetic and incomplete by design. It contains a small number of representative assets with estimated locations and replacement values. Its purpose is to demonstrate the exposure → vulnerability → loss arithmetic transparently, not to reproduce the corridor’s actual asset stock.

Limitations follow from that design:

- The table is not a real client submission and is not statistically representative of affected properties.
- Coordinates are present, but the loss calculation does not intersect assets with a hazard surface. Flood depths are assigned manually to each row.
- Losses apply only to the included assets and modelled mechanisms. They must not be extrapolated to corridor or national totals without a defensible inventory and scaling methodology that this project does not provide.

In production, exposure work would begin with completeness and consistency checks: missing or inconsistent occupancy and construction fields; replacement values in mixed currencies or valuation years; coordinates outside the study area; duplicate records; missing or inappropriate vulnerability classifications; and consistency between asset type and the assigned vulnerability function. The illustrative table stops short of that workflow so that every assumption in the loss calculation remains visible. What it demonstrates is structure; what it does not demonstrate is a validated portfolio inventory.

### 3.2 Vulnerability functions

Hazard intensity is translated into physical damage through simplified, literature-informed depth–damage functions. The implementation is informed by the global flood depth–damage framework of Huizinga, de Moel and Szewczyk (2017), but the curves used here are **linear placeholders**, not the published JRC functions reproduced without modification, and they are not calibrated to Nepali construction types, local damage observations or this event.

**Implemented form.** For each asset \(i\):

$$
D_i = \min(1, s_{t(i)} \cdot h_i)
$$

$$
L_i = R_i \cdot D_i
$$

where \(h_i\) is assigned flood depth (m), \(R_i\) is replacement value (USD), \(D_i\) is the damage ratio, \(L_i\) is direct physical ground-up loss, and \(s_{t(i)}\) is an asset-type slope:

Table 2. Illustrative depth–damage slopes
| Asset type | Illustrative slope |
|---|---|
| Residential | 0.15 |
| Commercial | 0.10 |
| Bridge | 0.20 |
| Hydropower | 0.25 |
| Road segment | 0.30 |

Damage ratios are bounded in \([0, 1]\). Unrecognised asset types, missing depths or negative depths yield NaN and are excluded from the total rather than treated as zero. Loss under these direct-damage assumptions cannot exceed replacement value. The calculation is fully reproducible from the exposure table and the function defined in `02_exposure_vulnerability/vulnerability_functions.py`.

**Worked example.** Asset A0005 (hydropower):

\( R = 50{,}000{,}000 \) USD, \( h = 2.5\,\mathrm{m} \), \( s = 0.25 \).

$$
D = \min(1,\ 0.25 \times 2.5) = 0.625
$$

$$
L = 50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.625 = 31{,}250{,}000 \text{ USD}
$$

**Comparison with other vulnerability-assessment approaches**

Depth–damage functions are one of several approaches used to assess the physical vulnerability of buildings and infrastructure. The main approaches differ in how they represent hazard intensity, asset characteristics and damage outcomes.

| Approach | What it represents | Relevance and limitations for this case |
|---|---|---|
| Flood depth–damage functions | Relate inundation depth to an expected damage ratio for a particular asset or building class. | Quantitative and relatively straightforward to apply where suitable depth data and damage functions are available, but less suitable where dynamic debris loading is important. |
| Debris-flow vulnerability matrices | Relate combinations of flow characteristics and asset characteristics to qualitative or categorical damage levels. | Can incorporate variables such as flow depth, velocity and building characteristics. Useful where data are insufficient to develop continuous damage curves, but results depend on classification criteria and expert judgement and may not translate directly into monetary loss. |
| Debris-flow vulnerability curves | Relate one or more measures of debris-flow intensity to the degree of physical loss. | Can produce quantitative damage estimates and incorporate intensity measures such as debris height, velocity or impact pressure. Their reliability depends on matched observations of hazard intensity, asset characteristics and damage; transferability between regions and building types is limited. |
| Infrastructure fragility functions | Estimate the probability that an asset reaches or exceeds a defined damage state under a specified hazard intensity. | Potentially relevant to bridges and other critical infrastructure. They require asset-specific structural information, defined damage states and suitable intensity measures. Their probabilistic damage-state outputs are not directly equivalent to the damage ratios produced by vulnerability functions. |
| Indicator-based approaches | Assess susceptibility using characteristics of the exposed asset, such as construction material, structural form and building configuration. | Can represent differences in asset characteristics that generic curves may overlook. However, indicator selection and weighting may involve expert judgement, and an indicator score does not automatically provide a monetary damage estimate. |

These approaches are not interchangeable, and no single method is universally preferable. Papathoma-Köhle et al. (2017) review the advantages and limitations of debris-flow vulnerability matrices, curves and indicators. Their comparison highlights the importance of detailed damage documentation, process-intensity information and asset characteristics, as well as the difficulty of transferring vulnerability relationships between different settings.

Multi-parameter intensity is not a separate method but the set of hazard inputs that vulnerability or fragility relationships can use. For debris floods these may include flow depth, velocity, sediment concentration, impact pressure and deposition or scour, depending on the process and the asset.

The present model retains a depth-only linear function as a transparent simplification, not as a representation of debris-flood loading. Research on debris-flow damage shows that losses depend on the interaction of flow depth with velocity, sediment concentration, boulder impact and scour (Jakob & Hungr 2005; Fuchs et al. 2007; Totschnig et al. 2011). Chamoli (Shugar et al. 2021) is an analogue for this process class, not evidence for the damage response of the August 2026 event. Using depth alone may therefore misrepresent damage to some asset classes, particularly bridges and hydropower works, but the direction and magnitude of any aggregate bias cannot be established without event-specific intensity and damage observations. The processes omitted, and the type of uncertainty each introduces, are listed below.

| Processes not represented             | Potential damage relevance                 | Uncertainty type            |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| Sediment concentration and deposition | Burial, inundation, sediment loading       | Epistemic (process form)    |
| Boulder transport and impact          | Localised structural failure               | Epistemic + aleatory        |
| Channel erosion and scour             | Foundation undermining, bridge failure     | Epistemic (parameter)       |
| Flow velocity and momentum            | Structural loading                         | Epistemic (hazard variable) |
| Debris blockage                       | Local water-level increase, flow diversion | Aleatory + epistemic        |

### 3.3 Ground-up loss result and what it represents

Applying the functions to the illustrative exposure table produces a ground-up loss of approximately **US$33.3 million** under the stated assumptions. This is a deterministic model output for the included synthetic assets, and it covers **direct physical damage** only. Business interruption and other indirect consequences are not included. Insurance terms are applied separately in Section 3.4.

### 3.4 Illustrative insured-loss demonstration

A separate financial layer applies hypothetical policy terms to the synthetic exposure table, to show how ground-up damage translates into gross insured and policyholder-retained loss. Under demonstration terms of a US$1 million deductible and a US$20 million limit per asset, the ground-up total of approximately US$33.3 million produces roughly US$20.6 million in gross insured loss and US$12.7 million in policyholder-retained loss.

The US$12.7 million is loss retained by policyholders under the hypothetical terms, not the insurer's net retained loss after reinsurance. The calculation uses no actual policy schedules, coverage conditions or reinsurance arrangements, and is not an estimate of the event's actual insured loss. One hydropower asset (A0005) accounts for most of both the ground-up and gross insured totals; this reflects the design of the synthetic table, not an observed concentration in the real corridor portfolio. A real estimate would need the inputs listed in Section 5.2.

## 4. Insurance, Claims, and the Protection Gap

This section examines how losses interact with Nepal’s insurance market and wider disaster financing.

The central distinction is between economic loss, insured loss, claims submitted, claims paid and recovery-financing needs. These measures are related but not interchangeable. Meaningful comparison requires consistent exposure coverage, hazard and vulnerability assumptions, and loss definitions. Those foundations are not aligned in the available evidence, so the comparisons below illustrate differences in scale and category rather than produce an insured-loss ratio or protection-gap estimate.

A further distinction is useful for interpreting the figures that follow. Calibration is the adjustment of model parameters so that outputs match observed data. Validation is the testing of a calibrated model against data not used in calibration. Benchmark comparison is the juxtaposition of model outputs with external figures that differ in scope, date, valuation basis or loss definition. The present analysis performs none of the first two. The external figures in Table 3 and Section 4 are retained as benchmark comparisons only. They are not used to adjust vulnerability slopes, assigned depths or any other model parameter.

### 4.1 Insurance exposure: hydropower and infrastructure

The flood affected a corridor in which hydropower generation, transmission infrastructure, roads and bridges are economically important and geographically concentrated. Hydropower is particularly relevant because of its replacement values, business-interruption exposure and dependence on interconnected infrastructure.

The Nepal Insurance Authority (NIA) report *Hydropower Insurance in Nepal: Assessment of Trends, Challenges, and Opportunities for Reform* provides useful context for the sector's existing insurance arrangements. Its findings point to significant underwriting and claims-management pressures in hydropower insurance.

The report identifies several issues:

* **Claims experience is volatile.** The reported hydropower claim ratio varied substantially between financial years, reflecting the influence of large losses and the relatively concentrated nature of the portfolio.
* **Underwriting performance is under pressure.** Using the report’s reported loss ratios and an assumed 5% expense ratio applied uniformly, a simple (unweighted) average of the available annual figures produces an estimated average gross combined ratio of approximately 146% and an estimated net combined ratio of approximately 108%. The report excludes FY2077/78 from the underlying data because it includes pre-MTR data and notes that FY2081/82 contains only nine months of data. The source does not state whether the loss ratios are incurred or paid, nor whether a premium-weighted calculation was used; the figures here are therefore labelled estimated.
* **Claims can take a long time to settle.** The report identifies a median settlement time of approximately 298 days in its sample of 121 paid claims, increasing to 372 days for Engineering All Risks (EAR) claims.
* **Claims assessment is an operational bottleneck.** The report identifies the survey and assessment process as a significant contributor to delays.

These findings should be interpreted as evidence of structural pressures in the hydropower insurance market, not as a direct measurement of the financial performance of insurers following the August 2026 flood. The combined ratios are derived estimates, not independently calculated market-wide results, and the claims-settlement sample is not necessarily representative of all claims.

The report also describes the use of different reinsurance arrangements. Its reported distribution includes treaty and facultative arrangements (62%), proportional or treaty arrangements (21%), facultative arrangements (16%), and a small residual category involving combined arrangements. These percentages reflect the report’s own classification of arrangements; the categories are not mutually exclusive (for example, proportional cover can itself be written as a treaty), and the source does not state the denominator (policies, projects or insurers). The figures should not be interpreted as a mutually exclusive market-share distribution of premiums ceded or losses recovered.

Taken together, the findings indicate that the flood struck a sector already facing questions about underwriting sustainability, claims administration and the availability of reinsurance for large infrastructure risks.

**Source:** Nepal Insurance Authority, *Hydropower Insurance in Nepal: Assessment of Trends, Challenges, and Opportunities for Reform* (2026).

### 4.2 Insurance claims following the August 2026 flood

The Nepal Insurance Authority's (NIA) reported claims figures provide an indication of the scale of the insurance response, although they remain provisional and subject to adjustment as loss assessments progress.

By 16 October 2026, insurers had reported approximately **NPR 20.9 billion in claims**, against which **NPR 821.39 million had been paid**. The amount paid represented approximately **3.9% of the reported claims total** at that date. This is a comparison of payments made against claims reported, not a final settlement rate or an estimate of ultimate insured losses.

The reported claims were distributed across several insurance classes:

Property insurance accounted for the largest reported claim value, while motor insurance generated the largest number of claims. Engineering and contractor insurance also represented a substantial share of the reported value, reflecting the importance of infrastructure and construction-related risks in the affected area.

The payment position varied by class. Reported payments included approximately NPR 238.9 million for transit claims, NPR 236.6 million for motor claims, NPR 174 million for engineering and contractor claims, NPR 162.8 million for property claims and NPR 8.9 million for other claims.

The low proportion paid by 16 October should not automatically be interpreted as the final proportion of insured losses that will be recovered. The event had occurred less than two months earlier, and large infrastructure claims require access to damaged sites, technical assessment, documentation, coverage review and, in some cases, reinsurance coordination. The figures are a dated snapshot of claims processing, not a final settlement ratio.

The three available reporting snapshots are retained as dated observations rather than treated as a continuous claims series (see Section 3.5 for the earlier figures). The accompanying counts refer to different reporting units, and the sources do not establish whether the reported amounts cover identical populations or reflect revised assessments and additional submissions. The figures therefore cannot be interpreted as a documented progression in ultimate claims liability.

### 4.3 Modelled loss and reported disaster effects: different measures

The illustrative model in Section 3 produces approximately **US$33.3 million in ground-up loss** under its current exposure, hazard and vulnerability assumptions. This figure applies only to the synthetic assets included in the model. It is not an estimate of total losses from the flood, nor is it a measure of insured losses.

For comparison, the Government of Nepal's September 2026 Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment (RDNA) estimates:

* **US$1.812 billion** in physical damage;
* **US$883 million** in economic losses;
* **US$2.695 billion** in total disaster effects; and
* **US$4.775 billion** in total recovery and reconstruction needs.

The distinction between these figures matters. Physical damage refers to damage to assets; economic losses capture the effects of disrupted economic activity and services. Total disaster effects combine these two categories. Recovery and reconstruction needs are higher because they include the resources required to restore and rebuild, including measures intended to improve resilience.

**Source:** Government of Nepal, *Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment: Rasuwa-Bhotekoshi Flood 2026* (September 2026). The RDNA figures are also summarised in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' press briefing of 11 September 2026 (Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2026).

### 4.4 The protection-gap question

The reported insurance claims total of NPR 20.9 billion as of 16 October 2026 can be set alongside the wider disaster estimates to illustrate the relative scale of formal insurance claims. Against the RDNA's NPR 408.3 billion in total disaster effects, the reported claims represent approximately **5.1%**. Against NPR 723.3 billion in recovery and reconstruction needs, they represent approximately **2.9%**.

These ratios are scale comparisons only, not estimates of the insured share of disaster losses or the protection gap. The numerator represents provisional claims submitted to insurers, while the denominators cover much broader categories of physical damage, economic losses and recovery expenditure. They therefore do not measure equivalent populations or financial obligations.

A defensible protection-gap estimate would require eligible insurable losses to be reconciled with actual policy coverage, insured asset values, deductibles, limits, exclusions, accepted claims and final insurer payments.

The protection gap is also not limited to the difference between insured and uninsured physical damage. It includes the financial consequences of business interruption, loss of livelihoods, damage to public infrastructure, disruption to essential services and reconstruction costs that fall outside private insurance arrangements.

For example, damage to a hydropower facility can generate direct physical repair costs as well as lost generation revenue. Whether those losses are insured depends on the policy structure, including whether business interruption cover is included, the applicable waiting period and the terms governing interruption caused by physical damage. The available aggregate claims figures do not establish how much of the event's business interruption losses will ultimately be insured or recovered.

Until claims data can be reconciled with asset-level damage and sectoral loss assessments, the available figures demonstrate the scale of the financing challenge rather than the uninsured share.

### 4.5 Beyond private insurance: public disaster-risk financing

Private insurance is only one layer of disaster-risk financing. Large-scale disasters also require public resources, contingent financing and international support, particularly for public infrastructure, emergency response and recovery needs that cannot be transferred readily to commercial insurers.

Nepal's existing financing arrangements include the World Bank-supported **US$150 million Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option (Cat DDO)**, approved in October 2024. The instrument provides contingent sovereign financing that can be accessed following an eligible disaster or emergency, subject to its conditions. It is a credit facility, not an insurance policy or grant; its approval should not be treated as evidence that the funds were drawn down for the August 2026 event.

Separately, on 23 September 2026, the World Bank stated that it had made available up to US$170 million in emergency financing to support the Government of Nepal’s immediate response and begin restoring critical infrastructure and services (World Bank, 2026).

These instruments illustrate the role of sovereign and development-partner financing in absorbing losses beyond the reach of private insurance. They also highlight the distinction between financing immediate response, restoring damaged assets and meeting longer-term reconstruction and resilience needs.

The RDNA's estimate of US$4.775 billion in recovery and reconstruction needs provides the broader context for this challenge. The scale of the requirement means that recovery cannot be understood solely through the amount of insurance claims submitted or paid.

### 4.6 Implications for catastrophe-risk analysis

The insurance evidence adds a financial dimension to the physical risk analysis in the preceding sections. Geographic accumulation and functional interdependency (Section 2.5) are the main drivers of correlated insured loss in this corridor, but neither is quantified here. Two further implications follow.

First, **gross loss does not determine the final financial burden**. The allocation of losses depends on policy coverage, applicable financial terms and reinsurance arrangements, as well as the extent of public and private risk retention.

Second, **claims settlement is part of the financial impact**. Delays between damage, assessment and payment can constrain the liquidity available to affected businesses and infrastructure operators. The NIA's hydropower findings suggest that this is a structural issue worth considering alongside the scale of the event's reported claims.

## 5. What the Analysis Can and Cannot Support

### 5.1 Strengths of the present analysis

Three features give the work its value.

- **Mechanism clarity and evidence-status discipline.** The event is reconstructed as a cascading hazard chain, with confirmed observations, reported interpretations and unresolved mechanisms explicitly distinguished.
- **Transparent arithmetic.** The illustrative loss calculation is reproducible, with explicit assumptions, documented inputs and defined treatment of invalid records.
- **Separation of loss categories.** Modelled loss, observed damage, reported claims and reconstruction needs are treated as distinct measures rather than forced into numerical agreement.

### 5.2 Limits

The analysis does not provide:

- A stochastic event catalogue or frequency model derived from this single event.
- Average annual loss (AAL), probable maximum loss (PML) or exceedance-probability (EP) curves.
- Application of real (as opposed to hypothetical demonstration) policy terms, limits, deductibles or reinsurance structures.
- A hydraulic or debris-flow model that would convert a hazard surface into spatially varying intensity at asset locations.
- A statistically representative or client-specific exposure inventory.

These omissions are deliberate scope boundaries, not temporary data gaps that the present repository claims to close.

A production workflow would require georeferenced assets with defensible values, an intensity representation appropriate to the dominant damage processes, and vulnerability functions suitable for the relevant asset classes and construction context. Insurer applications would additionally require portfolio-level policy and claims data, while public risk-finance applications would require information on public assets, fiscal exposure and existing contingent financing arrangements.

### 5.3 Epistemic and aleatory uncertainty in this event

The residual uncertainty in this analysis is predominantly epistemic.

Epistemic uncertainty arises from incomplete knowledge or imperfect model representation. In this case it includes the unresolved source location and volume, the uncertain status of any transient landslide dam, uncalibrated vulnerability parameters, and incomplete representation of the physical processes governing damage.

Aleatory uncertainty refers to inherent variability in outcomes under a specified model and set of conditions. Even with a defined hazard intensity field and vulnerability function, realised damage may still vary because of local differences in asset response and physical processes. An information gap about whether boulders reached a particular asset is epistemic; probabilistic representation of variation in a known boulder-transport process may be treated as aleatory.

Both forms of uncertainty can exist simultaneously. Better observations, exposure data, process understanding and calibration can reduce epistemic uncertainty, although they cannot necessarily eliminate it. Aleatory variability is characterised and quantified through probabilistic modelling rather than removed through additional data.

In this case study, neither form of uncertainty is quantified probabilistically. A further distinction is useful. Uncertainty about exposure, hazard intensity and vulnerability concerns the model itself. Differences in geographical coverage, reporting dates, loss definitions and assessment methodologies between the present calculation and external figures are primarily comparability limitations. They are related to, but not the same as, model uncertainty.

The distinction between epistemic and aleatory uncertainty follows the uncertainty typology commonly applied in catastrophe-model governance (Grossi & Kunreuther 2005).

### 5.4 Comparison with a rapid-mapping product (EMSR927)

As noted in Section 2.3, EMSR927 is a rapid-mapping product for emergency response, not a loss model (Copernicus EMSR927 Grading product, 27 August 2026).

## 6. Implications

### 6.1 Recommendations for insurers, reinsurers and public risk-finance institutions

The analysis points to five practical priorities for assessing risk in a high-concentration mountain corridor.

1. **Build geocoded inventories that include access roads and shared infrastructure**, not only the insured asset. Accumulation and interruption exposure depend on what lies along the same valley floor.
2. **Record dependencies between assets**, such as which road or bridge a powerhouse relies on, so that functional interdependency (Section 2.5) can be assessed rather than assumed.
3. **Use intensity measures beyond flood depth** where data allow, since depth alone does not capture the loading mechanisms of a debris flood (Section 3.2).
4. **Keep loss categories separate**: physical damage, operational interruption, insured loss, claims reported, claims paid and public recovery needs answer different questions. They should not be treated as equivalent measures or used to infer an insured share without reconciliation (Section 4).
5. **Test accumulation across asset classes and portfolios**, including whether one event can trigger claims under property, engineering, motor and transit classes at once (Table 4).
6. **Review event definitions and access-related extensions in policy and reinsurance wordings.** The unresolved timing of the initiating failure matters for event definition: a 25 August versus 26 August reconstruction could affect the application of hours clauses and whether losses are aggregated as one or more occurrences under the relevant treaty wording. Damage to access roads also raises a coverage question where an insured asset remains operationally inaccessible despite no physical damage to the asset itself. Denial-of-access and contingent business-interruption extensions may address different aspects of this exposure, subject to their specific triggers and policy terms.

For public risk-finance, commercial insurance addresses only part of a corridor-wide loss. Public infrastructure, household livelihoods and informal activity need separate financing, which sovereign contingent facilities (Section 4.5) can address only in part.

### 6.2 Further analytical extensions

**Next: deterministic sensitivity analysis.**

Define a small deterministic sensitivity set that varies the assumed intensity assigned to selected assets and the vulnerability slopes, and report the resulting loss range. The result would be a sensitivity analysis of the illustrative model, not an uncertainty range for actual event losses. A separate hypothetical higher-intensity case can illustrate financial sensitivity but should not be presented as a physically modelled transient-dam breach.

**Future: spatial exposure screening.**

Replace manual depth assignments with a spatial intersection between an openly available asset layer and a defined affected-area footprint, overlaying a defensible hazard-intensity raster where one exists. EMSR927 grading can support screening and comparison but is not a substitute for flood-depth or debris-flow intensity surfaces.

## 7. Conclusion

The August 2026 Nepal–China border flood-debris cascade exposed a high-concentration mountain corridor to a hazard whose dominant processes remain only partly resolved. The research question was what an insurer, reinsurer or public risk-finance institution would need to understand about the catastrophe risk revealed by this event.

The analysis establishes the structure of that risk. A high-altitude rock/ice slope failure generated a debris flood that reached the Rasuwagadhi border area within minutes and propagated along the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli corridor. Hydropower, transport, border facilities and settlements share a narrow valley, creating the potential for correlated losses across asset classes. The illustrative calculation shows how a ground-up loss workflow can be built transparently, while the external damage, economic-loss, reconstruction and claims figures provide context but remain distinct measures. The financial consequences remain insufficiently quantified for underwriting or fiscal risk assessment, for the reasons set out in Section 5.2.

The principal contribution is therefore not an estimate of the disaster's financial impact but a framework for identifying what can be quantified, what remains uncertain, and what evidence is required before the event can be treated as a modelled catastrophe risk. The recommendations in Section 6.1 set out where that evidence should come from.
